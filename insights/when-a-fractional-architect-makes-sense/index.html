Fractional Architecture · Perspective

When a fractional architect makes sense, and when it doesn’t

Between having no architect and hiring a full-time principal, there is a gap that many growing engineering organisations fall into. A fractional architect can fill it, but only for the right problems. Here is how to tell the difference.

Intervencija26 October 20264 min read

Architecture becomes a problem gradually. One team becomes three, then six. A monolith gains satellite services. Integrations multiply. Decisions that used to happen across a desk now span teams with different priorities, and nobody is explicitly responsible for how the pieces fit together. The CTO is the default owner, but the CTO is also hiring, budgeting, managing stakeholders and keeping delivery on track.

At that point many organisations consider hiring a principal or chief architect. That is often the right long-term answer. It is also a senior hire that takes months to make, and the need may be uneven: intense during a modernisation or a platform decision, lighter in between. A fractional architect, meaning a senior architect engaged for a defined share of their time, is one way to bridge that gap.

Signals that it could help

A fractional architect tends to add the most value when several of these are true:

  • Decisions span teams, and nobody owns the whole. Teams make locally sensible choices that do not fit together, and the integration cost appears later.
  • The same debates keep returning. Without written decisions, questions about databases, service boundaries or messaging are reopened every few months.
  • A major change is running alongside feature delivery. A modernisation, migration or new platform needs consistent technical direction while the teams keep shipping.
  • Integration sprawl is growing. New partners, APIs and data flows are added faster than anyone can keep them coherent.
  • Technical leadership is stretched. The CTO or head of engineering needs a senior sounding board, and someone who can go deep on design while they lead the organisation.
  • An external review is coming. Due diligence, an enterprise customer’s security assessment or an audit needs credible architecture documentation and clear answers.

When it is the wrong answer

A fractional architect is not a universal remedy. It is usually the wrong choice when:

  • The real problem is delivery capacity. If the architecture is sound and the team simply has more work than people, you need engineers, not architecture.
  • You need daily, hands-on leadership of a large team. Some situations need someone present every day. A full-time hire or an interim lead is a better fit.
  • Nobody internally will own the decisions. A fractional architect advises, facilitates and documents, but decisions have to be owned by people who are there every day. Without that, recommendations evaporate.
  • The goal is a verdict rather than a partner. Architecture guidance works when teams help shape it. Imposed from outside, it is quietly ignored.

How to structure the engagement

The engagements that work best share a few characteristics.

A clear remit and clear decision rights

Agree in writing what the architect is responsible for, which decisions they make, which they recommend, and which stay with your leaders. Ambiguity here causes more friction than any technical disagreement.

A predictable rhythm

A typical rhythm combines a standing weekly slot for design reviews, asynchronous review of proposals between sessions, and a monthly review of technical priorities with leadership. Predictability matters more than volume. Teams plan around the slot, and decisions do not wait for someone to become available.

Outputs that outlast the engagement

The value should be visible in artefacts the organisation keeps: architecture decision records, a maintained view of the system landscape, proportionate standards, and a technical roadmap linked to business priorities. If the engagement ended tomorrow, these should remain useful.

A plan for its own end

A good engagement builds internal capability. Over time, your engineers should write more of the decision records and lead more of the reviews. Revisit the remit every quarter: expand it during a major change, reduce it as internal ownership grows, or hand over to a permanent hire with the groundwork already in place.

Questions to ask before you engage one

  1. Which decisions have you owned in systems like ours, and what changed as a result?
  2. How do you work with a team that disagrees with a recommendation?
  3. What will we have in writing after the first month?
  4. How will we know whether the engagement is worth continuing?
  5. What does handing over to a permanent architect look like?

If the answers are concrete, the engagement is likely to be too.
